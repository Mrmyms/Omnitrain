\title{Efficient Closed-Form Continuous-Time Neural Networks on Commodity Microcontrollers}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Manuel Yobani Martinez Sanchez}
\IEEEauthorblockA{OmniTrain Research Group}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Deploying sequence-modeling neural networks on deeply embedded hardware (TinyML) remains challenging due to the strict SRAM constraints of microcontrollers and the temporal jitter inherent to physical sensors. Traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) such as LSTM and GRU assume discrete, synchronous time steps and are typically deployed through schema-heavy execution frameworks that incur substantial dynamic memory overhead. We present an end-to-end systems-engineering pipeline for the native, bare-metal deployment of Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) neural networks on highly constrained hardware (ESP32-S3). A custom Execute-in-Place (XIP) parameter-mapping architecture (\texttt{.omnibit}) eliminates SRAM copies of network weights, bypassing traditional framework overhead entirely. Evaluated against standard LSTM and GRU deployments, our pipeline demonstrates resilience to sensor jitter on a Processor-in-the-Loop (PiL) Inverted Pendulum benchmark, mathematical parity with full-precision (FP32) PyTorch models (MSE~$<10^{-6}$), lower inference latency, and a reduced SRAM and energy footprint. All pipelines and the C++ inference engine are open-sourced for reproducibility.\footnote{Source code and hardware exporter available at: \url{https://github.com/mrmyms/Omnitrain}}
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
TinyML, Continuous-Time Neural Networks, Edge Computing, Execute-in-Place, Embedded Systems, Microcontrollers

\section{Introduction}
The proliferation of autonomous edge robotics has driven demand for localized, low-latency artificial intelligence. Processing time-series data on-device ensures deterministic response times, preserves privacy, and reduces dependency on cloud infrastructure. However, state-of-the-art sequence models remain fundamentally misaligned with embedded hardware in two respects.

First, discrete-time models such as LSTM and GRU degrade sharply under irregular sampling: packet loss and clock jitter desynchronize their internal state from the physical process. Approaches such as Phased LSTM \cite{neil2016phased} or Neural CDEs \cite{kidger2020ncde} address temporal irregularity but remain computationally heavy for microcontrollers. Second, such models are typically deployed through generalized inference engines (e.g., TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers), whose dynamic memory arenas consume a large share of the SRAM available on MCUs, while heavy memory-access patterns raise dynamic power draw.

In this work we bring Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) architectures \cite{hasani2022cfc} to bare-metal execution through a novel deployment pipeline. Rather than proposing a new architecture, our systems-engineering contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A bare-metal C++ inference engine for continuous-time sequence models, optimized natively for Xtensa architectures.
\item An Execute-in-Place (XIP) tensor-mapping architecture (\texttt{.omnibit}) that maps Flash memory directly to the CPU cache, bypassing SRAM allocation for parameters.
\item Benchmarking showing CfCs execute faster, consume less energy, and are more robust to sensor degradation than equivalent LSTMs and GRUs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Continuous-Time Neural Networks}
Neural ODEs \cite{chen2018node,rubanova2019latent} and Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks \cite{hasani2021ltc}, inspired by the \textit{C. elegans} nervous system \cite{lechner2020ncp}, introduced continuous-time dynamics into recurrent networks. Hasani et al.\ \cite{hasani2022cfc} later derived a closed-form approximation (CfC), but its execution characteristics on deeply embedded hardware remain underexplored.

\subsection{Embedded ML and Memory Bottlenecks}
TinyML research \cite{warden2020tinyml} largely targets model size via INT8 quantization; we hypothesize this degrades the dynamic range that differential equations require. Hardware-aware NAS \cite{cai2018proxyless} and ahead-of-time compilers such as microTVM or TinyEngine/MCUNet \cite{lin2020mcunet} improve execution speed but lack native primitives for continuous-time neural networks, and Spiking Neural Networks \cite{roy2019snn} require specialized neuromorphic hardware. Execute-in-place memory mapping itself has precedent in NAND-based mobile embedded systems \cite{kim2014nandxip}, though not previously applied to continuous-time neural inference. Critically, deploying continuous-time formulations on discrete frameworks like TFLite Micro \cite{david2021tflitemicro} forces the mathematical kernel into schema-heavy scalar operations, causing catastrophic overhead and memory blow-up \cite{banbury2021mlperftiny}. Our static XIP memory map avoids this, keeping the active SRAM footprint low enough to retain full FP32 precision.

\section{Methodology: The OmniEngine Architecture}
\subsection{The CfC Mathematical Kernel}
We implement the closed-form CfC formulation of Hasani et al.\ \cite{hasani2022cfc}. The hidden state $x(t)$ evolves under input $I(t)$ via the Liquid Time-Constant ODE
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx(t)}{dt} = -\left(\frac{1}{\tau} + f(x(t), I(t); \theta_f)\right)x(t) + A\,f(x(t), I(t); \theta_f)
\label{eq:ltc}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the baseline time-constant vector, $f(\cdot)$ a nonlinear decay/gating network parameterized by $\theta_f$, and $A$ a learned steady-state bias. Approximating the decay integral gives the closed-form update over an irregular physical interval $\Delta t$:
\begin{IEEEeqnarray}{rCl}
x(t) &=& x(t-\Delta t)\odot\bigl(1 - f(x(t-\Delta t), I(t); \theta_t)\bigr) \nonumber\\
&& {}+\, g(x(t-\Delta t), I(t); \theta_g)\odot f(x(t-\Delta t), I(t); \theta_t) \label{eq:update}
\end{IEEEeqnarray}
where $g(\cdot)$ (parameters $\theta_g$) is the state backbone producing the candidate hidden state, $f(\cdot)$ (parameters $\theta_t$) is the time-gate, and $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product. A Sigmoid activation bounds $f(\cdot)$ to $(0,1)$ so that \eqref{eq:update} is a valid convex combination, limiting sensitivity to extreme $\Delta t$ at inference -- though gate saturation can still induce vanishing gradients during training, which we mitigate with careful initialization and AdamW. Because $\Delta t$ is the true physical time elapsed since the last sample, the time-gate scales the state update by the actual elapsed time rather than assuming a fixed step, which is what lets the model absorb irregular sampling natively.

\subsection{XIP Tensor Mapping and Cache Optimization}
The \texttt{.omnibit} format is produced by a Python toolchain that extracts the PyTorch \texttt{state\_dict}, serializes tensors deterministically, and emits a binary payload for direct ROM flashing (Table~\ref{tab:omnibit}). Using SPI Flash memory mapping, the C++ engine performs matrix multiplications by fetching weights directly from ROM through the hardware data cache rather than SRAM (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Reading from SPI Flash is inherently slower than SRAM and prone to cache misses, but the row-major, contiguous layout of \texttt{.omnibit} lets the Xtensa LX7's hardware prefetcher exploit spatial locality and minimize miss penalties.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Hardware and Energy Measurement}
Experiments used an ESP32-S3 (dual-core Xtensa LX7, 512\,KB SRAM) prototyped on a solderless breadboard; despite the breadboard's parasitic capacitance, the \texttt{.omnibit} payload ran reliably from external SPI Flash at 80\,MHz in Quad I/O mode (production deployments still require controlled PCB routing to avoid EMI and signal-integrity issues). Steady-state current draw (MCU plus Flash) was logged with an INA219 power monitor at 1\,kHz, and microsecond-scale transients were captured with a digital storage oscilloscope across a low-tolerance shunt resistor.

\subsection{Dataset and Task Definition}
We evaluate on a Processor-in-the-Loop (PiL) Inverted Pendulum / CartPole \cite{barto1983cartpole} simulation. Although LQR \cite{astrom2010feedback} gives optimal control at negligible memory cost for this linearized task, CartPole is used here purely as a benchmarking environment that isolates temporal robustness from confounds such as nonlinearity (Section~V-E extends to unmodeled friction and noise). Neural policies output forces sequentially from the physical state (position, velocity, angle, angular velocity). The control loop runs at 50\,Hz ($\Delta t = 0.02$\,s); models are trained and evaluated on 5{,}000-step sequences, split 70/15/15 (train/val/test), with sensor inputs z-scored using training-set statistics. All models train only on clean, contiguous data (0\% loss); test streams are then masked with 0/20/60\% random packet loss, holding the last valid sample via Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) during drops.

\subsection{Baselines and Training Protocol}
Baselines are a Time-Aware LSTM (4{,}120 params) and Time-Aware GRU (3{,}980 params), both receiving $\Delta t$ concatenated to $I(t)$ to mitigate temporal misalignment; our CfC has 4{,}050 params. All models share $d_{in}=5$, $d_{model}=32$, $d_{out}=1$, and the CfC backbone uses $d_{backbone}=64$. Baselines are deployed via TFLite Micro \cite{david2021tflitemicro}. Training used PyTorch with AdamW \cite{kingma2014adam,loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 1, 250 epochs, Huber loss) on an NVIDIA RTX~5070 GPU, across 30 random seeds, reporting mean $\pm$ standard deviation of MSE.\footnote{Software stack: PyTorch 2.2.0, CUDA 12.1, NumPy 1.24.3, Arduino ESP32 Core 2.0.11 (PlatformIO \texttt{espressif32}). A Docker container is provided in the repository to guarantee bit-exact reproducibility of the \texttt{.omnibit} payload across host operating systems.}

\section{Results and Evaluation}
\subsection{Temporal Resilience}
The primary task metric is Time-to-Failure (TTF, capped at 500 steps) before the pendulum or cart leaves the safe envelope. At 20\% packet loss the LSTM and GRU baselines collapsed to an average TTF of 92.9 and 106.8 steps; at 60\% loss the LSTM fell to 50.0 steps. The CfC instead sustained 277.7 steps at 20\% loss and 257.3 steps at 60\% loss (Fig.~\ref{fig:ttf}). To rule out an artifact of evaluating on out-of-distribution noise, we augmented LSTM training with 20\% ZOH packet loss; even then it reached only 135.8 ($\pm$164.5) steps at 20\% test loss -- still far below the zero-shot CfC. A Mann-Whitney $U$ test across the 30 seeds confirms this gap is highly significant ($p=1.2\times10^{-4}$; Holm-Bonferroni-adjusted $p_{adj}<0.001$, $m=3$ \cite{holm1979bonferroni}; large effect size $d\approx1.41$ \cite{cohen1988power}), consistent with the heavy-tailed errors produced by catastrophic pendulum failures.

\subsection{Hardware-in-the-Loop Simulation with Real Sensor Jitter}
To test execution under real bus conditions, we ran a Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) simulation \cite{macchelli2022hil} in which a physical I2C MPU6050 supplied genuine bus jitter and interrupt overhead while simulated CartPole states were injected into the pipeline. The OmniEngine generated high-frequency PWM control signals without triggering watchdog resets or stack overflows, confirming resilience to I2C interrupt latency; this open-loop test does not yet evaluate closed-loop robustness, which we leave to future work.

\subsection{Hardware Efficiency: Memory, Latency, and Power}
Table~\ref{tab:hw} summarizes hardware utilization. XIP removes the active tensor SRAM footprint: all 16.5\,KB of FP32 parameters live in mapped Flash, trading SRAM for D-cache miss penalties. Since the ESP32-S3's 32\,KB D-cache exceeds the combined 16.5\,KB ROM + 6.1\,KB SRAM working set, the steady-state cache-hit ratio approaches 100\% after the initial cold miss, largely hiding Flash latency. The 6.1\,KB SRAM allocation is fixed and fragmentation-free, used only for activation buffers and transient state; the CfC's own state footprint is a mere 1.2\,KB ($d_{model}=32$), versus TFLite Micro's 42.5\,KB dynamic arena for an equivalent LSTM. Stack profiling via FreeRTOS \texttt{uxTaskGetStackHighWaterMark} shows under 800\,B of stack depth, avoiding overflow risk during asynchronous interrupts.

By avoiding FlatBuffer schema traversal, the CfC reaches 3.43\,ms/step, with power stabilizing at 52\,mW (60\,mW cold-start peak) versus the TFLite LSTM's 105\,mW (idle baseline: 38\,mW). To isolate architecture from engineering, we built an ``LSTM-XIP'' baseline on the same bare-metal payload: it needs only 6.1\,KB SRAM but reaches just 6.15\,ms/step at 65\,mW, showing that XIP alone substantially improves on TFLite Micro, while the CfC's fewer per-step gate evaluations still give it a further latency and power edge.

\subsection{Silicon Parity and the FP32 Advantage}
Unlike INT8 post-training quantization commonly used to fit SRAM budgets, our XIP architecture lets the CfC solver run entirely in FP32 on the Xtensa LX7, since quantization is suspected to compress the dynamic range that continuous-time dynamics need. This preserves mathematical parity between the PyTorch training environment and the microcontroller (MSE~$<10^{-6}$) without quantization-induced instability.

\subsection{Generalization to Unmodeled Dynamics}
To test beyond ideal, linear CartPole kinematics, we evaluated the CfC (trained only on frictionless data) under simulated cart/pole friction ($\mu_{cart}=0.1$, $\mu_{pole}=0.05$), Gaussian sensor noise ($\sigma=0.01$), and 20\% packet loss simultaneously -- a genuine out-of-distribution stress test. The CfC still sustained an average TTF of 206.6 ($\pm$113.8) steps, suggesting its temporal extrapolation ability generalizes beyond idealized kinematics.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Baseline framework overhead.} We isolated XIP's memory gains from the CfC's mathematical advantage using an LSTM-XIP baseline, but extending native XIP to all discrete RNN variants (e.g., GRU) remains future work.
\item \textbf{Scalability ceiling.} The ESP32-S3's small D-cache (32/64\,KB) means models beyond $\sim$50{,}000 parameters ($>$200\,KB in FP32) would overflow it, causing cache thrashing and non-linear latency degradation.
\item \textbf{Task complexity.} Thirty seeds give strong statistical confidence on CartPole, but higher-dimensional benchmarks (e.g., multi-axis drone stabilization) are needed to map the CfC solver's scalability boundary.
\item \textbf{Extreme jitter saturation.} As $\Delta t \to \infty$ under prolonged packet loss, the time-gate saturates and the state decays to the learned bias $A$ -- stable, but only a neutral prediction; mapping this saturation boundary is left for safety-critical future work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
This paper shows that CfC networks are practical for severely constrained embedded systems: Zero-Copy XIP memory semantics let engineers deploy temporally resilient, energy-efficient architectures without sacrificing FP32 precision. Future work will package the C++ OmniEngine as a micro-ROS node for direct integration with flight controllers such as PX4 or ArduPilot, and will run scalability ablations to determine the parameter budget at which SPI Flash bandwidth becomes the binding constraint.
