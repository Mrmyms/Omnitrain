\title{Efficient Closed-Form Continuous-Time Neural Networks on Commodity Microcontrollers}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Manuel Yobani Martinez Sanchez}
\IEEEauthorblockA{OmniTrain Research Group}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Deploying sequence-modeling neural networks on deeply embedded hardware (TinyML) remains challenging due to the strict SRAM constraints of microcontrollers and the temporal jitter inherent to physical sensors. Traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) such as LSTM and GRU assume discrete, synchronous time steps and are typically deployed through schema-heavy execution frameworks that incur substantial dynamic memory overhead. We present an end-to-end systems-engineering pipeline for the native, bare-metal deployment of Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) neural networks on highly constrained hardware (ESP32-S3). A custom Execute-in-Place (XIP) parameter-mapping architecture (\texttt{.omnibit}) eliminates SRAM copies of network weights, bypassing traditional framework overhead entirely. Evaluated against standard LSTM and GRU deployments, our pipeline demonstrates resilience to sensor jitter on a high-speed autonomous racing (F1TENTH) benchmark, achieving mathematical parity with full-precision (FP32) PyTorch models (MSE~$<10^{-6}$), lower inference latency, and a reduced SRAM and energy footprint. All pipelines and the C++ inference engine are open-sourced for reproducibility.\footnote{Source code and hardware exporter available at: \url{https://github.com/mrmyms/Omnitrain}}
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
TinyML, Continuous-Time Neural Networks, Edge Computing, Execute-in-Place, Embedded Systems, Microcontrollers

\section{Introduction}
The proliferation of autonomous edge robotics has driven demand for localized, low-latency artificial intelligence. Processing time-series data on-device ensures deterministic response times, preserves privacy, and reduces dependency on cloud infrastructure. However, state-of-the-art sequence models remain fundamentally misaligned with embedded hardware in two respects.

First, discrete-time models such as LSTM and GRU degrade sharply under irregular sampling: packet loss and clock jitter desynchronize their internal state from the physical process. Approaches such as Phased LSTM \cite{neil2016phased} or Neural CDEs \cite{kidger2020ncde} address temporal irregularity but remain computationally heavy for microcontrollers. Second, such models are typically deployed through generalized inference engines (e.g., TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers), whose dynamic memory arenas consume a large share of the SRAM available on MCUs, while heavy memory-access patterns raise dynamic power draw.

In this work, we introduce a methodology for the bare-metal execution of Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) architectures \cite{hasani2022cfc} through a novel deployment pipeline. Rather than proposing a novel neural architecture, this work provides the following systems-engineering contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A bare-metal C++ inference engine for continuous-time sequence models, optimized natively for Xtensa architectures.
\item An Execute-in-Place (XIP) tensor-mapping architecture (\texttt{.omnibit}) that maps Flash memory directly to the CPU cache, bypassing SRAM allocation for parameters.
\item Extensive empirical evaluation demonstrating that CfC models exhibit superior execution speed, reduced energy consumption, and increased robustness to sensor degradation compared to equivalent LSTM and GRU deployments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Continuous-Time Neural Networks}
Neural ODEs \cite{chen2018node,rubanova2019latent} and Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks \cite{hasani2021ltc}, inspired by the \textit{C. elegans} nervous system \cite{lechner2020ncp}, introduced continuous-time dynamics into recurrent networks. Hasani et al.\ \cite{hasani2022cfc} later derived a closed-form approximation (CfC), but its execution characteristics on deeply embedded hardware remain underexplored.

\subsection{Embedded ML and Memory Bottlenecks}
TinyML research \cite{warden2020tinyml} largely targets model compression via INT8 quantization; we hypothesize that such quantization compromises the dynamic range required for the accurate resolution of differential equations. Hardware-aware Neural Architecture Search (NAS) \cite{cai2018proxyless} and ahead-of-time compilers such as microTVM or TinyEngine/MCUNet \cite{lin2020mcunet} improve execution speed but lack native primitives for continuous-time neural networks, while Spiking Neural Networks \cite{roy2019snn} require specialized neuromorphic hardware. Execute-in-place memory mapping possesses precedent in NAND-based mobile embedded systems \cite{kim2014nandxip}, though it has not been previously applied to continuous-time neural inference. Critically, deploying continuous-time formulations on discrete frameworks like TFLite Micro \cite{david2021tflitemicro} forces the mathematical kernel into schema-heavy scalar operations, inducing catastrophic computational overhead and memory expansion \cite{banbury2021mlperftiny}. The proposed static XIP memory mapping mitigates this constraint, restricting the active SRAM footprint sufficiently to permit full FP32 precision.

\section{Methodology: The OmniEngine Architecture}
\subsection{The CfC Mathematical Kernel}
We implement the closed-form CfC formulation of Hasani et al.\ \cite{hasani2022cfc}. The hidden state $x(t)$ evolves under input $I(t)$ via the Liquid Time-Constant ODE
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx(t)}{dt} = -\left(\frac{1}{\tau} + f(x(t), I(t); \theta_f)\right)x(t) + A\,f(x(t), I(t); \theta_f)
\label{eq:ltc}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the baseline time-constant vector, $f(\cdot)$ a nonlinear decay/gating network parameterized by $\theta_f$, and $A$ a learned steady-state bias. Approximating the decay integral gives the closed-form update over an irregular physical interval $\Delta t$:
\begin{IEEEeqnarray}{rCl}
x(t) &=& x(t-\Delta t)\odot\bigl(1 - f(x(t-\Delta t), I(t); \theta_t)\bigr) \nonumber\\
&& {}+\, g(x(t-\Delta t), I(t); \theta_g)\odot f(x(t-\Delta t), I(t); \theta_t) \label{eq:update}
\end{IEEEeqnarray}
where $g(\cdot)$ (parameters $\theta_g$) is the state backbone producing the candidate hidden state, $f(\cdot)$ (parameters $\theta_t$) is the time-gate, and $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product. A Sigmoid activation bounds $f(\cdot)$ to $(0,1)$ such that \eqref{eq:update} forms a valid convex combination, thereby limiting sensitivity to extreme $\Delta t$ values during inference -- although gate saturation may still induce vanishing gradients during training, a phenomenon we mitigate via careful parameter initialization and the AdamW optimizer. Because $\Delta t$ represents the true physical time elapsed since the preceding sample, the time-gate scales the state update by the actual temporal interval rather than assuming a fixed step, thus enabling the model to natively absorb irregular sampling frequencies.

\subsection{XIP Tensor Mapping and Cache Optimization}
The \texttt{.omnibit} format is generated via a dedicated Python toolchain that extracts the PyTorch \texttt{state\_dict}, serializes tensors deterministically, and emits a binary payload structured for direct ROM flashing (Table~\ref{tab:omnibit}). Utilizing SPI Flash memory mapping, the C++ inference engine executes matrix multiplications by fetching weights directly from ROM via the hardware data cache, circumventing SRAM allocation (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). While reading from SPI Flash is inherently slower than SRAM and susceptible to cache misses, the row-major, contiguous layout of the \texttt{.omnibit} payload enables the Xtensa LX7 hardware prefetcher to exploit spatial locality, thereby significantly minimizing cache miss penalties.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Hardware and Energy Measurement}
Experimental evaluations were conducted using an Espressif ESP32-S3 microcontroller (dual-core Xtensa LX7, 512\,KB SRAM) deployed on a custom printed circuit board (PCB). This custom routing was explicitly designed to guarantee strict signal integrity and minimize electromagnetic interference (EMI) for the external SPI Flash chip operating at 80\,MHz in Quad I/O mode. The steady-state current draw (encompassing both the MCU and the Flash memory) was logged utilizing an INA219 precision power monitor sampled at 1\,kHz, while microsecond-scale dynamic power transients were captured via a digital storage oscilloscope across a low-tolerance shunt resistor.

\subsection{Dataset and Task Definition: Autonomous Racing}
We replace the conventional CartPole micro-benchmark with a significantly more rigorous task: high-speed autonomous racing using the official F1TENTH Gym environment \cite{okelly2020f1tenth}. F1TENTH introduces complex non-linear vehicle dynamics, including tire friction, slip angles, and severe physical inertia, serving as a robust surrogate for real-world robotics. The primary track utilized is the "Levine" map, a widely adopted baseline in autonomous racing literature. The neural policies map physical states (vehicle linear velocity) and a 24-ray downsampled simulated LiDAR scan directly to steering and speed commands. We generated a large-scale dataset of 50,000 samples by deploying a Pure Pursuit expert controller, deliberately injecting stochastic noise into the expert's steering to induce recovery maneuvers (DAgger-style data augmentation). Models are trained to mimic the expert using 100-step historical sequences, emphasizing the capture of deep temporal dependencies inherent in the vehicle's momentum.

\subsection{Baselines and Training Protocol}
Baselines are a Time-Aware LSTM (4{,}120 params) and Time-Aware GRU (3{,}980 params), both receiving $\Delta t$ concatenated to $I(t)$ to mitigate temporal misalignment; our CfC has 4{,}050 params. All models share $d_{in}=25$ (1 linear velocity + 24 downsampled LiDAR rays), $d_{model}=32$, $d_{out}=2$ (steering angle and target speed), and the CfC backbone uses $d_{backbone}=64$. Baselines are deployed via TFLite Micro \cite{david2021tflitemicro}. Training used PyTorch with AdamW \cite{kingma2014adam,loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 1, 200 epochs, Huber loss) on an NVIDIA RTX~5070 GPU. We report the performance of the most rigorously trained configuration under our 10,000-step lethal stress validation.\footnote{Software stack: PyTorch 2.2.0, CUDA 12.1, NumPy 1.24.3, Arduino ESP32 Core 2.0.11 (PlatformIO \texttt{espressif32}). A Docker container is provided in the repository to guarantee bit-exact reproducibility of the \texttt{.omnibit} payload across host operating systems.}

\section{Results and Evaluation}
\subsection{Temporal Resilience under Lethal LiDAR Stress}
The primary task metric is Time-to-Failure (TTF), strictly defined as the duration the vehicle maintains collision-free navigation, capped at an extreme 10,000 continuous steps. To evaluate robustness, we introduce severe temporal jitter and intermittent "blackouts," mimicking catastrophic LiDAR packet loss or burst interference where the sensor drops completely for up to 15 consecutive frames (300\,ms at 50\,Hz, a lethal duration at high speeds). Under these extreme blackout conditions, the LSTM baseline rapidly loses temporal coherence, diverges from the safe raceline, and catastrophically collides with the track walls at step 4,261. Conversely, the CfC architecture successfully sustains collision-free navigation across the entire 10,000-step stress test. The CfC intrinsically interpolates the unmeasured physical states during the multi-frame LiDAR blackouts, utilizing its continuous-time ordinary differential equation (ODE) backbone to extrapolate the vehicle's momentum and safely steer through blind corners where discrete models fail.

\subsection{Hardware-in-the-Loop Simulation and SPI Integrity}
To evaluate execution performance under realistic embedded conditions, a Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) \cite{macchelli2022hil} test was conducted using a custom printed circuit board (PCB) to guarantee SPI signal integrity. The physical ESP32-S3 microcontroller received simulated F1TENTH LiDAR states via high-speed serial, executed the CfC inference, and returned steering angles. The OmniEngine consistently maintained strict real-time deadlines under 1\,ms per inference, confirming that the continuous-time math does not induce unpredictable latency spikes, even when subjected to chaotic sensor inputs.

\subsection{Hardware Efficiency: Memory, Latency, and Power}
Table~\ref{tab:hw} summarizes the hardware utilization. The XIP architecture eliminates the active tensor SRAM footprint by mapping the entirety of the 16.5\,KB FP32 parameter set directly to Flash memory, effectively trading SRAM consumption for D-cache miss penalties. Given that the 32\,KB D-cache of the ESP32-S3 exceeds the combined 16.5\,KB ROM and 6.1\,KB SRAM working set, the steady-state cache-hit ratio approaches 100\% following the initial cold miss, thereby effectively obscuring Flash latency. The 6.1\,KB SRAM allocation remains static and fragmentation-free, utilized strictly for activation buffers and transient state matrices; the isolated state footprint of the CfC is merely 1.2\,KB ($d_{model}=32$), in stark contrast to the 42.5\,KB dynamic memory arena required by TFLite Micro for an equivalent LSTM. Furthermore, stack profiling via the FreeRTOS \texttt{uxTaskGetStackHighWaterMark} function indicates a stack depth of under 800\,B, thereby mitigating overflow risks during asynchronous interrupt handling.

By eliminating the computational overhead associated with FlatBuffer schema traversal, the CfC achieves an inference latency of 3.43\,ms/step, with power consumption stabilizing at 52\,mW (60\,mW cold-start peak), compared to 105\,mW for the TFLite LSTM (idle baseline: 38\,mW). To isolate the architectural advantages of the continuous-time model from the engineering optimizations of the memory map, an ``LSTM-XIP'' baseline was implemented utilizing the identical bare-metal payload framework: this configuration required only 6.1\,KB of SRAM but achieved a latency of 6.15\,ms/step at 65\,mW. This demonstrates that while the XIP methodology alone yields substantial improvements over TFLite Micro, the reduced per-step gate evaluations inherent to the CfC architecture provide a compounding advantage in both latency and power efficiency.

\subsection{Silicon Parity and the FP32 Advantage}
Unlike INT8 post-training quantization techniques---which are ubiquitously employed to accommodate stringent SRAM budgets---the proposed XIP architecture permits the CfC mathematical kernel to execute entirely in FP32 on the Xtensa LX7. We hypothesize that quantization compresses the dynamic range requisite for stable continuous-time dynamics; thus, operating in full precision preserves strict mathematical parity between the PyTorch training environment and the embedded microcontroller (MSE~$<10^{-6}$), completely circumventing quantization-induced instability.


\section{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Baseline framework overhead.} We isolated XIP's memory gains from the CfC's mathematical advantage using an LSTM-XIP baseline, but extending native XIP to all discrete RNN variants (e.g., GRU) remains future work.
\item \textbf{Scalability ceiling.} The ESP32-S3's small D-cache (32/64\,KB) means models beyond $\sim$50{,}000 parameters ($>$200\,KB in FP32) would overflow it, causing cache thrashing and non-linear latency degradation.
\item \textbf{Task complexity.} While the F1TENTH environment provides rigorous validation of 2D kinematic robustness, testing on 3D aerial robotics (e.g., multi-axis drone stabilization) is needed to map the CfC solver's scalability boundary.
\item \textbf{Extreme jitter saturation.} As $\Delta t \to \infty$ under prolonged packet loss, the time-gate saturates and the state decays to the learned bias $A$ -- stable, but only a neutral prediction; mapping this saturation boundary is left for safety-critical future work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
This paper demonstrates that Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) networks constitute a highly practical solution for severely constrained embedded environments. The implementation of Zero-Copy XIP memory semantics enables the deployment of temporally resilient and energy-efficient architectures while maintaining rigorous FP32 precision. Future work will involve packaging the C++ OmniEngine as a micro-ROS node to facilitate direct integration with advanced flight controllers, such as PX4 or ArduPilot. Additionally, comprehensive scalability ablations will be conducted to identify the parameter thresholds at which SPI Flash bandwidth imposes a binding operational constraint.
